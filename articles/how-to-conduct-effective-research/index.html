How to Conduct Effective Research: From Questions to Reliable Findings

By Ezeikpe Akachukwu Kingsley (Kabaka)September 18, 20266 min read

Research is not something reserved for university professors, scientists or people working in laboratories. Whenever you investigate a question, examine available information and try to establish what is true, you are engaging in research.

A student preparing an academic project, a nurse investigating a clinical question and a writer verifying information for an article all need the same important skill: knowing how to find information, judge its quality and use it responsibly.

The challenge is that information is everywhere. Books, websites, social media, journals and online discussions can provide answers, but not everything you read is accurate. Effective research is therefore not about collecting the largest amount of information. It is about finding information that genuinely helps answer your question.

1. Start with a clear research question

Before opening Google or downloading articles, identify exactly what you want to investigate.

For example, "Nursing" is too broad to serve as a focused research question. You could narrow it to "How does effective communication influence patient understanding of medication instructions?"

The second question gives you a clearer direction. You now know that you need information about communication, patient understanding and medication instructions.

A good research question should be specific enough to investigate, but broad enough to allow you to discover useful information.

2. Know where to look for information

Different research topics require different sources. You do not have to depend on one website or one search engine.

  • Google Scholar: Useful for discovering academic articles, research papers and scholarly publications.
  • PubMed: Particularly useful for biomedical and healthcare research.
  • University libraries: Helpful for books, academic resources and research guidance.
  • Government and recognised institutional websites: Useful for official reports, policies, statistics and guidelines.
  • Academic journals: Useful for examining studies and evidence related to a specific topic.

Remember that finding a source does not automatically make it reliable. You still need to examine who produced it and what evidence supports its claims.

3. Learn to recognise reliable information

One of the most important research skills is evaluating sources. An attractive website, confident author or widely shared social media post does not guarantee accuracy.

Before using information, ask yourself:

  • Who wrote it? Is the author identifiable, and do they have relevant knowledge or experience?
  • Where was it published? Is the publisher or institution trustworthy?
  • When was it published? Is the information recent enough for your topic?
  • What evidence supports it? Does the author provide references, data or explanations that can be checked?
  • Why was it written? Is the purpose to inform, persuade, advertise or promote a particular viewpoint?

For example, if you are researching a current healthcare recommendation, an old online article may no longer reflect current guidance. You should look for more recent and authoritative evidence.

4. Do not collect information without organising it

Research becomes confusing when you download dozens of documents, open multiple browser tabs and forget where important information came from.

Create a simple research notebook or document. For every useful source, record:

  • Author or organisation.
  • Title of the article or publication.
  • Year of publication.
  • Website, journal or publisher.
  • Important findings and how they relate to your question.
  • Link or reference details for later citation.

Keep your own explanations separate from the original author's words. This helps you understand what you have learned and makes it easier to acknowledge other people's work correctly.

5. Compare information before drawing conclusions

Do not accept an important claim simply because you found it in one article. Look for other credible sources discussing the same issue.

Sometimes, different studies reach different conclusions. This does not necessarily mean that one must be false. They may have studied different populations, used different methods or examined the problem under different conditions.

Your responsibility is to examine the evidence, consider the differences and avoid presenting an uncertain conclusion as an established fact.

6. Present your findings honestly

After gathering and examining information, organise your findings around your original research question. Explain what the evidence shows, identify important limitations and make conclusions that are supported by what you actually discovered.

Always acknowledge the sources of information you use. Copying another person's words or presenting their ideas as your own is plagiarism. When using an exact quotation, identify it as a quotation and provide the appropriate reference. When explaining an idea in your own words, cite the original source where required.

A simple research exercise

Choose a topic that interests you, such as the relationship between sleep and academic performance.

  1. Write a clear question about the topic.
  2. Find three relevant sources from credible organisations or academic publications.
  3. Record the author, publication date and important findings from each source.
  4. Compare what the sources say and identify any differences.
  5. Write a short explanation of what you learned, acknowledging your sources.

This small exercise introduces you to the process of asking questions, finding evidence, evaluating information and communicating findings.

Final thoughts

Research is a skill that improves with practice. You do not need to begin with a complicated project or understand every research method immediately. Start with a question, look for credible information, examine the evidence and organise what you discover.

Most importantly, learn to question information rather than accepting everything you encounter. A good researcher is not simply someone who knows where to find information. A good researcher knows how to examine it, recognise its limitations and use it responsibly.

Do not research merely to collect information. Research to understand, investigate and discover what the evidence can genuinely tell you.

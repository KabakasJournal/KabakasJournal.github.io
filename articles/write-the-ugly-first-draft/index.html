Write the Ugly First Draft: Why You Must Stop Editing Before You Finish

By Ezeikpe Akachukwu Kingsley (Kabaka)October 28, 20266 min read

For years, I had a habit that looked like dedication but was quietly destroying my writing progress. Whenever I wrote a sentence, I wanted it to be perfect. I would change a word, rearrange a paragraph, delete an entire section and start again. Sometimes, I spent more time correcting the opening paragraph than developing the story itself.

The strange part was that I felt productive. I was writing, after all. But weeks could pass, and I would still be standing at the beginning of the same piece. I had become so concerned with making every sentence beautiful that I had forgotten the most important responsibility of a writer: finishing the work.

That experience taught me something important. Sometimes, the biggest obstacle between a writer and a completed piece is not a lack of talent, inspiration or ideas. It is the inability to allow an imperfect beginning.

Your first draft has a different responsibility

A first draft is not the final product. It is the raw material from which the final product will emerge. Its responsibility is to capture your thoughts, establish your direction and give your ideas somewhere to live outside your imagination.

Think about building a house. You do not begin by polishing the walls, selecting the perfect decorations or inspecting the final paintwork. You begin with a foundation and a structure. Writing works in much the same way.

Your first draft may contain weak sentences, repeated ideas, awkward transitions and paragraphs that eventually need to disappear. That is not evidence that you cannot write. It is evidence that you are still developing the material.

Finish the story first. Then discover what it needs to become.

Why perfectionism keeps writers stuck

Perfectionism often disguises itself as a commitment to quality. You tell yourself that you are improving the work, but repeatedly editing unfinished sections can interrupt your thinking and make it difficult to move forward.

There are three common problems with this habit:

  • It breaks creative momentum. Every time you stop to correct a sentence, you move your attention away from the larger idea you were trying to develop.
  • It creates unnecessary pressure. When every sentence must sound impressive, writing begins to feel like an examination instead of a creative process.
  • It makes completion difficult. You can spend hours improving the same few paragraphs while the rest of the article, chapter or book remains unwritten.

There is nothing wrong with wanting excellent work. The problem is demanding final-draft quality from a first-draft process. Excellence requires revision, but revision needs something to work on.

Separate writing from editing

One of the most useful habits a writer can develop is learning to treat drafting and editing as two different activities.

During drafting, your responsibility is to develop ideas and move the work forward. During editing, your responsibility is to examine those ideas, improve their expression, correct errors and remove unnecessary material.

Trying to perform both activities at the same time can make the process unnecessarily difficult. Give yourself permission to write badly before you attempt to write beautifully.

Five practical rules for writing your first draft

You do not need an elaborate writing system. Start with a few simple rules and practise them consistently.

  1. Write for 25 minutes without editing. Set a timer and concentrate on moving forward. Do not return to previous paragraphs simply because a sentence could sound better.
  2. Use placeholders instead of stopping. If you cannot remember a date, quotation or technical detail, write [check this later] and continue. Research can happen during revision.
  3. Follow the idea before judging the language. If a paragraph communicates what you mean, leave it alone for now. You can improve its expression when the draft is complete.
  4. Accept imperfect sections. Some paragraphs will be stronger than others. Some ideas may not work. Keep moving until you have enough material to evaluate the entire piece.
  5. Leave yourself a starting point for tomorrow. Before ending a writing session, make a brief note about what should happen next. This reduces the effort required to return to the work.

What if you do not know what to write next?

Sometimes, the difficulty is not perfectionism. You simply do not know how to continue. When this happens, resist the temptation to abandon the entire piece.

Ask yourself three questions:

  • What am I trying to communicate in this section?
  • What does the reader need to understand before I move forward?
  • What is the next logical point, example or event?

If you are writing fiction, think about what your character wants, what is preventing them from getting it and what might happen next. If you are writing an educational article, consider the next question your reader is likely to ask.

You do not always need the perfect next sentence. Sometimes, you only need to understand the next idea.

When should you start editing?

Once you have completed the draft, take a short break before reviewing it. Returning with fresh eyes makes it easier to recognise weaknesses that were difficult to notice while writing.

Do not attempt to correct everything at once. Review your work in stages:

  • First review: Structure. Does the piece have a clear beginning, middle and conclusion? Are the ideas arranged logically?
  • Second review: Meaning. Are your explanations clear? Have you supported important points with examples where necessary?
  • Third review: Language. Improve sentence construction, remove repetition and strengthen weak expressions.
  • Final review: Accuracy. Check spelling, grammar, punctuation, facts, names and references.

This approach gives your writing room to develop. You are no longer trying to create a perfect piece in one sitting. You are building something through deliberate stages.

A small exercise to change your writing habit

Try this today. Choose one topic you have been postponing and write for 25 uninterrupted minutes. Do not edit, delete entire sections or search for the perfect opening sentence.

When the timer ends, save your work. Read it only to understand what you have produced, not to criticise every sentence. Then identify one clear next step for your next writing session.

You may discover that you have written more than you expected. Even if the result is rough, you now have something real to improve.

The courage to finish

Writers need discipline, patience and a willingness to improve. But they also need the courage to create something imperfect and continue working despite its imperfections.

Your first draft does not have to impress anyone. It does not have to sound like a published book, a professional article or the work of your favourite author. It only needs to move your idea from your mind onto the page.

The ugly draft is not the enemy of good writing. It is often the beginning of it. Give your ideas room to grow, finish what you start and trust the revision process to help you shape the work.

You cannot edit a story that you were too afraid to finish writing.

So, the next time you sit down to write, resist the urge to make the first sentence perfect. Write the next one. Then another. Keep going until you have something worth revising.

Write first. Improve later. Finish the work.
